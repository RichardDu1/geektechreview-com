The Elgato Wave DX is the dynamic microphone that does the boring things correctly: a tight cardioid pattern that rejects room noise, a locking shock mount, a tap-to-mute capacitive surface, and a built-in pop filter. At $149 it undercuts the Shure SM7B by $250 and embarrasses most USB streaming mics in its price range.

## The capsule

The Wave DX is an XLR-only dynamic microphone with a cardioid polar pattern. Dynamic (not condenser) is the right choice for untreated rooms — it rejects off-axis sound and ambient noise far better than the condenser mics most streamers mistakenly buy. The capsule has a warm, broadcast-friendly frequency response with a presence boost in the 4-8kHz range that brings vocals forward without harshness. Proximity effect is pronounced — get close and your voice deepens satisfyingly.

It requires an audio interface with XLR input and phantom-free preamp gain. Pair it with the Elgato Wave XLR or a GoXLR/Focusrite Scarlett and you're set. Budget interfaces may struggle to provide enough clean gain for a dynamic mic — the Wave DX is not unusually gain-hungry, but dynamics always need more preamp than condensers.

## The hardware

The body is all-metal, dense, and reassuringly heavy. The signature feature is the locking shock mount — a swing-arm mechanism that isolates the capsule from desk thumps and keyboard vibration. Most streaming mics bundle flimsy suspension mounts; Elgato's is sturdy and effective. A built-in pop filter handles plosives (P, B) well without an external screen.

The capacitive top panel is tap-to-mute with an RGB indicator ring (red = muted, configurable colors). There's also a volume knob on the mic itself — by default it controls the headphone monitoring level, but it's reprogrammable via Elgato's software.

## Sound in practice

In a treated-ish bedroom with a mechanical keyboard 30cm away, the Wave DX rejects the keyboard noise to a faint click — a condenser would capture every keystroke. Voice sounds full and broadcast-ready with minimal processing. A little EQ (high-pass at 80Hz, +2dB at 5kHz) and light compression and you have a podcast-quality chain for under $250 total.

Compared to the Shure SM7B: the Wave DX is slightly brighter, slightly less full in the low-mids, and lacks the SM7B's legendary build — but it's 60% of the price and 85% of the sound. Compared to USB condenser mics at the same price (HyperX QuadCast, etc.), the Wave DX wins on room rejection and broadcast character, provided you already have an interface.

## Software

Elgato's Wave Link software is the killer app — a virtual mixing board that lets you route Discord, game audio, music, and mic into separate channels with per-source monitoring and outputs. You don't need the Wave DX to use Wave Link, but it's the natural pairing. The software is free and genuinely excellent.

## What's wrong

- XLR-only. If you want USB, look at the Wave:3 — but you'll sacrifice the dynamic capsule's room rejection.
- Requires a preamp/interface with sufficient clean gain — budget interfaces may introduce hiss.
- The shock mount's swing arm has a learning curve to position correctly.
- No included stand or boom arm — budget another $50-100.
- Capacitive mute can be accidentally triggered; you can disable it in software.
- The RGB ring is garish if you don't want RGB on your mic.

## The verdict

The Wave DX is the streaming microphone that streamers should have been recommended from the start. It's a dynamic capsule in a well-engineered package at a price that makes the SM7B look indulgent. Pair it with an interface and Wave Link and you have a broadcast chain for less than a single Shure. If you're serious about your audio and tired of sounding like you're in a tin can, this is the upgrade.
